--- Is constant ε sufficient? ---

  Best constant-ε results:
    SARSA  : constant ε=0.1, α=0.2 → -288.35
    Q-Lrn  : constant ε=0.1, α=0.2 → -341.13

  Best decaying-ε results:
    SARSA  : decay 1→0.01, d=0.995, α=0.1 → -264.27
    Q-Lrn  : decay 1→0.01, d=0.99,  α=0.1 → -293.61

  Answer: No, a constant ε is NOT sufficient. Decaying ε consistently
  outperforms constant ε for both algorithms:
    - SARSA improvement:      -288.35 → -264.27  (Δ = +24.08)
    - Q-Learning improvement: -341.13 → -293.61  (Δ = +47.52)

  Reason: With constant ε, the agent continues taking random actions
  (10% or 30% of the time) even after learning a good policy. These
  random actions waste steps and accumulate negative reward (-1 per step).
  Decaying ε starts with high exploration (ε=1.0) to discover good
  trajectories, then reduces to near-zero (0.01) so the agent can fully
  exploit its learned policy. This explore-then-exploit strategy is
  essential for convergence to a near-optimal policy.

  Implemented decay schedule: ε_new = max(ε_min, ε_old × decay_rate)
  Best decay rates found: 0.995 (SARSA) and 0.99 (Q-Learning).

PART 2b — Multi-Seed Runs (10 seeds, tuned hyperparameters)
================================================================================

  Tuned hyperparameters used:
    SARSA      : α=0.1, ε: 1.0 → 0.01, decay=0.995
    Q-Learning : α=0.1, ε: 1.0 → 0.01, decay=0.99

  Per-seed training time: ~72s each (3,000 episodes)

  Final performance (mean return over last 200 episodes, across 10 seeds):
    SARSA      : -321.47 ± 14.29
    Q-Learning : -318.43 ± 34.18

--- Explanation of Results ---

  Both algorithms show clear learning curves, improving from ~-500 (random
  policy) towards ~-300 over 3,000 episodes.

  SARSA and Q-Learning achieve similar final performance (-321 vs -318),
  with overlapping confidence bands. However, they differ in key ways:

  1. Convergence speed: Q-Learning tends to improve slightly faster in
     early episodes because its update target (max Q) is always optimistic,
     pushing Q-values upward more aggressively.

  2. Stability: SARSA shows tighter confidence bands (std=14.29 vs 34.18),
     meaning it is more consistent across seeds. This is because SARSA's
     on-policy updates learn the value of the policy it actually follows
     (including ε-greedy exploration), making it less sensitive to the
     randomness of specific trajectories.

  3. Q-Learning's higher variance comes from its off-policy nature: it
     optimises for the greedy policy while behaving with ε-greedy. This
     mismatch can cause overestimation of Q-values, leading to more
     variable outcomes depending on which states are visited.

  4. As ε decays to near 0, both algorithms converge towards similar
     policies since the gap between on-policy and off-policy updates
     shrinks (when ε≈0, the ε-greedy policy ≈ the greedy policy).
